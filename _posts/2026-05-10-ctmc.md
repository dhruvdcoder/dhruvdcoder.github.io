## Overview

In this post, we will go over the basics of continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs).
The main goal is to provide an intuitive view of CTMCs.

{% details Prerequisites %}
This post assumes a basic understanding of:

- probability (random variables, expectation, conditional probability)
- calculus (limits, derivatives, integrals)
- discrete-time Markov chains (transition matrices, stationary distributions)
{% enddetails %}


## Poisson Processes

The Poisson process is the simplest CTMC, and provides most of the intuition needed for the general case.

### Counting process definition

{% definition Counting process %}
A counting process $C_t$ is a [stochastic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_process) that:

1. takes values in the non-negative integers, i.e., $C_t \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$;
2. is non-decreasing: $C_t \geq C_{t^{\prime}}$ for $t \geq t^{\prime}$.
{% enddefinition %}

A counting process is said to have **independent increments** if the number of events occurring in disjoint time intervals are independent.
That is, $C(t+s) - C(t)$ is independent of $C(t)$: the number of events between $t$ and $t+s$ is independent of the number of events between time $0$ and $t$, and vice versa.

A counting process is said to have **stationary increments** (also called time-homogeneous or simply homogeneous) if increments depend only on the length of the time interval and not on its position.
That is, for all $t_1, t_2, s > 0$ and $n \geq 0$,

$$
\Prob\bigl(C(t_1+s) - C(t_1) = n\bigr) = \Prob\bigl(C(t_2+s) - C(t_2) = n\bigr).
$$

Together, independent and stationary increments mean that increments on disjoint intervals are IID.

### Poisson process

{% definition Poisson Process %}
A counting process $N(t)$ is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ if <d-cite key="rossStochasticProcesses2007"></d-cite>:

1. $N(0) = 0$, i.e., $\Prob(N(0) = 0) = 1$;
2. it has independent and stationary increments;
3. for small $t$, $\Prob(N(t) = 1) = \lambda t + o(t)$;
4. for small $t$, $\Prob(N(t) \geq 2) = o(t)$,

{% enddefinition %}

where $o(t) = \\{ f \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{R}} \mid \lim_{t \to 0} f(t)/t = 0 \\}$, and in (3) and (4) the notation $o(t)$ means any function from this set.
One can also show that the following definition is equivalent.

{% definition Poisson Process (2) %}
A counting process $\\{N(t), t > 0\\}$ is said to be a **Poisson process** with rate $\lambda$ if <d-cite key="rossStochasticProcesses2007"></d-cite>:

1. $N(0) = 0$ a.s., i.e., $\Prob(N(0) = 0) = 1$;
2. it has independent increments;
3. $\Prob(N(s+t) - N(s) = n) = e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^n}{n!}$ for all $s, t > 0$.
{% enddefinition %}

That is, the number of events in an interval of length $t$ has a Poisson distribution with rate $\lambda t$.
Note that (3) implies stationary increments.
The third requirement is not as easy to verify as the first two, so we will also give an equivalent infinitesimal definition from which the Poisson distribution falls out via approximation to the binomial distribution.

### Poisson approximation of the Binomial

The Binomial distribution models the number of heads in a sequence of $n$ independent coin tosses with success probability $p$:

$$
p(k; n, p) = {n \choose k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}.
$$
For large $n$, this can be approximated by the Poisson distribution with rate $\lambda = np$.

To see this, expand the binomial pmf and take the limit as $n \to \infty$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(k; n, \lambda/n)
  &= \frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{k!} \frac{\lambda^k}{n^k}\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n-k} \\
  &= \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\left(\frac{n}{n}\frac{n-1}{n}\cdots \frac{n-k+1}{n}\right)\left(1+\frac{-\lambda}{n}\right)^n \left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k} \\
  &\implies \lim_{n \to \infty} p(k; n, \lambda/n)= \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \exp(-\lambda).
\end{aligned}
$$

So for large $n$, the binomial distribution with $p = \lambda/n$ is approximately Poisson with rate $\lambda$.
Letting $p = \lambda/n$ means that the probability of an individual head slowly reduces to $0$ as $n \to \infty$.



### Inter-arrival times

Let $T$ be the arrival time of the first event.
Then $\\{T > t\\} = \\{N(t) = 0\\}$, so

$$
\Prob(T \leq t) = 1 - \Prob(N(t) = 0) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}.
$$

The CDF of the first arrival time is therefore that of an exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$.

If $T_n$ and $T_{n+1}$ are the arrival times of the $n$-th and $(n+1)$-th events, then by independence and stationarity,

$$
\\{T_{n+1} - T_n > t\\} = \\{N(T_n + t) - N(T_n) = 0\\} = \\{N(t) = 0\\}.
$$

So all inter-arrival times $\tau_n = T_{n+1} - T_n$ are exponentially distributed.
Moreover, because $\\{N(T_n + \tau_n) - N(T_n)\\}$ and $\\{N(T_{n+1} + \tau_{n+1}) - N(T_{n+1})\\}$ are independent (where $T_{i+1} = T_i + \tau_i$), we have by stationarity that $N(\tau_n) \perp N(\tau_{n+1})$.
Therefore all inter-arrival times are independent.

The same argument applied to an arbitrary $s > 0$ gives $\\{N(\tau_i + s) - N(s)\\} \perp \\{N(s)\\}$, which tells us that the distribution of $\tau_i$ must be memoryless—another characterization of the exponential distribution.

Renewal processes generalize Poisson processes by allowing arbitrary IID inter-arrival times; the Poisson process is the special case with exponential inter-arrivals.
For more background on exponential and gamma random variables, see Durrett <d-cite key="durrettEssentialsStochasticProcesses2016"></d-cite>.

### Non-homogeneous Poisson processes

If we drop the requirement of stationary increments and generalize the infinitesimal conditions, we obtain the **non-homogeneous Poisson process** <d-cite key="rossStochasticProcesses2007"></d-cite>.

A counting process $N(t)$, equivalently a counting measure $N(s, t]$, is a non-homogeneous Poisson process with rate $\lambda(t)$ if:

1. $N(0) = 0$, i.e., $\Prob(N(0) = 0) = 1$;
2. it has independent increments;
3. $\displaystyle\lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{\Prob(N(t, t+h] = 1)}{h} = \lambda(t)$;
4. $\displaystyle\lim_{h \downarrow 0} \frac{\Prob(N(t, t+h] > 1)}{h} = 0$.

### One-event Poisson process

This is an important special case used throughout the literature on discrete flow matching and discrete diffusion.

Assume we have bounded time $t \in [0, 1]$ and we want to model the arrival time of a single event such that the event is guaranteed to happen by $t = 1$.
What is the most flexible way of modeling this using a non-homogeneous Poisson process (more generally, as a memoryless process)?

1. Pick a density and CDF for the arrival time $T$ such that $F(1) = 1$ and $F(0) = 0$.
2. Construct a non-homogeneous Poisson process $N$ with rate $\lambda(t)$ such that the law of the first arrival time matches $F(t)$.

The main question is: what should the hazard rate $\lambda(t)$ be?
For a non-homogeneous Poisson process, the survival probability is $S(t) = e^{-\int_0^t \lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}$, which should equal $1 - F(t)$.
Differentiating both sides gives

$$
\lambda(t) = \frac{f(t)}{1 - F(t)}.
$$

We can also write the density of the first event in terms of the hazard rate:

$$
f(t) = \lambda(t)(1 - F(t)) = \lambda(t) S(t) = \lambda(t) e^{-\int_0^t \lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s}.
$$

More generally, this construction can be used to build Markov processes that match more complex joint distributions over sequential events.
For example, suppose we are interested in a sequence of $n$ events represented by random variables $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ and arrival times $T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_n$, where each $X_i$ takes values in some space $[0, 1] \times \mathcal{X}$.
We have chosen a joint distribution $P(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n, T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_n)$ and want to construct a Markov process whose path measure matches this joint distribution.
If we take the time derivative of the joint distribution and plug it into the Kolmogorov forward equation to read off the rate matrix of the Markov process, would this require something more flexible, like a Markov renewal process?

### Time change

Denote by $\Lambda(t) = \int_0^t \lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$.
Let $U_1 := \Lambda(T_1)$, where $T_1$ is the first arrival time.
Since $\Lambda(t)$ is increasing and differentiable, the density of $U_1$ is given by the pullback of the density of $T_1$ under the map $u = \Lambda(t)$:

$$
f_{U_1}(u) = f_{T_1}(t) \frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}u} = \lambda(t) e^{-\int_0^t \lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s} \frac{1}{\Lambda^{\prime}(t)} = e^{-u}.
$$

Therefore in warped time $u = \Lambda(t)$, the first arrival time is exponentially distributed with rate $1$.

As a special case, let $\lambda(t) = a\,g(t)$ and set $u = w(t) = \int_0^t g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$.
Then

$$
f_U(u) = f_T(t) \frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\mathrm{d}u} = \lambda(t) e^{-\int_0^t \lambda(s)\,\mathrm{d}s} \frac{1}{w^{\prime}(t)} = a g(t) e^{-a w(t)} \frac{1}{g(t)} = a e^{-a u}.
$$
